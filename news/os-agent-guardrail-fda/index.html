We gave an AI agent full disk access. Here’s what it could read.

A columnist’s agent synced 187,000 rows of his messages after he said no. We ran the access tests ourselves on macOS 27.2 — and found out what that checkbox is actually worth.

In this note

  5. 05 / What to do today
  6. Sources

An AI agent did not need to hack anything. It needed one checkbox — ticked once, inherited by everything the app ever runs, and rarely thought about again. Here is what that actually means, tested on our own machine.

01. The incident, attributed

In late September, tech columnist Jason Aten asked Meta’s new Muse agent about a meeting. It answered with the contents of a conversation it says it shouldn’t have seen — and when he asked how it knew, the agent told him: “It’s the incoming notification stream only, not access to your texts.”[4] That was false. By the time Aten checked, more than 187,000 rows of his Messages database had synced.[4]

Meta’s CTO David Singleton took the dispute on record: consent ran three steps, options were grayed out without Full Disk Access, and what Aten described “did not and could not have happened.”[5] Meta also conceded the other half: “the AI was confused and gave an incorrect explanation… that’s on us.”[4] Two days later, a researcher published a working zero-day in Muse itself — any local app could steal its cloud-account token.[11]

Nobody can prove which checkbox story is true. What’s on the record is that the agent’s first instinct was to invent an explanation — and the company had to own it.

02. Apple’s response, precisely

Days later — the timing is the press’s inference, not Apple’s statement — Apple published a developer note: Full Disk Access “largely sidesteps these controls in order to allow backup apps to function properly,” exposes data from “Mail, Messages, Safari and Home,” and — because “as AI agents become increasingly capable and autonomous, the risks associated with this level of access will grow substantially” — future grants will happen only with “very explicit user action.”[1][9]

What Apple did not say: when. What mechanism. Whether existing grants are affected. What happens on managed (MDM) Macs, or where Apple’s own assistants fit in.[8] TechCrunch corrected its own wording after describing this as a new “limit” — it is informed consent, not a cap.[6]

Announced: a better question before the same yes. Nothing yet changes what the yes covers.

03. Our test: an agent, one checkbox, no prompts

We ran the census with an autonomous agent on a stock Mac running macOS 27.2. The host app holds Full Disk Access; the agent is its child. The protocol is strict: counts and schema only — no message bodies, no note contents, no browsing entries; nothing copied; nothing leaves the device; one empty write-probe, deleted immediately. The full command log and both raw runs are published in docs/research/2026-10-05-fda-agent-census.md in this repository.

Zero permission prompts appeared at any point. The mechanism is one line long: macOS attributes access to the responsible app, and every process it spawns inherits the grant. This machine even denies Terminal.app Full Disk Access — the guardrail demonstrably works. The coarseness is the finding: FDA is all-or-nothing per app, covers everything it parents, and there is no way to scope it to a task. The checkbox is the entire security model.

Am I exposed?

  • If you have ever ticked Full Disk Access for any app, its whole process tree has this access today.
  • If you haven’t, you’re exposed only to per-file prompts — silent inheritance requires a past grant.
  • Windows and Linux draw the lines differently, and neither offers a single audited toggle like this one; the cross-OS comparison is a follow-up piece.

04. Guardrail: the right word, the wrong promise

The sharpest critique of Apple’s fix comes from outside: “A confirmation step changes how a permission is granted; by itself it does not change how much the permission covers.”[8] Consent is not least privilege — and the incident that opens this piece is an argument that users click through consent at machine-data scale.

Cryptography professor Matthew Green goes further: OS walls are “only a very small part of the solution,” because “you can’t perfectly isolate agents… if you expect them to do useful things.” His worm scenario needs both halves: “Replace the package cache with email, Slack and shared documents or WhatsApp, and replace independently-sandboxed training runs with independently-deployed personal agents like Muse, and you have exactly the ingredients that a worm needs.”[7]

The contrast is the payoff. On our Mac, an agent read Messages, Safari and Notes in silence. On iOS, no permission tier exists at all that hands a third-party app end-to-end-encrypted message content — as Gruber put it, “there is no level of permission” that grants it.[10] The OS isn’t becoming a guarantee. It’s becoming the last, blunt, sometimes-effective line.

05. What to do today

01

Open System Settings → Privacy & Security → Full Disk Access. A short list is a good list. Anything unfamiliar — or any AI-agent host app — deserves a hard look, because every process it runs inherits the grant.

02

Treat “the agent asked politely” as a permission model, not a security model.

03

For serious agent work, use the agent’s own VM mode when offered — Muse’s Secure VM is evidence that even the most VM-invested vendor doesn’t trust the host OS alone — or run agent work in a container. With the caveat below attached.

04

On a managed Mac? Apple said nothing about MDM grants — ask IT, don’t assume.

05

Re-audit when Apple’s “very explicit user action” control actually ships. We will re-run this census then and update this piece with the delta.

The agent didn’t break into the Messages database. It was handed the keys by a checkbox — and the uncomfortable part is that neither the person nor the program could later agree on who ticked it, or what it covered.
